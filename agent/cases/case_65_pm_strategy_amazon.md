# Case File 65 — Amazon: Expanding Same-Day Pharmacy Delivery (PM Strategy & Growth Case)

## A. Case prompt (read aloud to candidate at the start — nothing else revealed yet)

"You're a PM on Amazon's pharmacy business. Today, prescription medicines are mostly delivered in two days, with same-day delivery available in a few large cities. Leadership is debating whether to expand same-day pharmacy delivery to 20 more cities. It's expensive, and pharmacy is a regulated business. They want your recommendation. How would you approach this?"

**Objective (private reference, reveal only if asked directly):** Recommend whether and how to expand same-day pharmacy delivery, based on customer needs, economics, and strategic value.

---

## B. Clarifying-question answer key

| # | Likely question | Answer | Reveal rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What's the goal?" | Grow the pharmacy business and make Amazon customers' go-to pharmacy. | On request |
| 2 | "Which prescriptions need same-day?" | Reveal Exhibit 1 once asked about prescription types or customer needs. | On request |
| 3 | "What does same-day cost?" | Reveal Exhibit 2 once asked about costs or economics. | On request |
| 4 | "What happened in cities that already have same-day?" | Reveal Exhibit 3 once asked about results in existing cities. | On request |
| 5 | "Who are the competitors?" | Local drugstore chains, where customers can pick up urgent prescriptions within hours. | On request |
| 6 | "What does a pharmacy customer generate in revenue?" | About $600 a year in gross profit for a customer who fills all their prescriptions with Amazon. | On request |
| 7 | "Are there regulatory constraints?" | Each state requires licensed pharmacy facilities and pharmacists; new local facilities take 6-12 months to open. | On request |

**Guardrail reminder:** Do not tell the candidate whether to expand, where, or how fast -- let them reason from the data.

## C. Exhibits / data

**Exhibit 1 — Prescription Mix**

| Type | Share of prescriptions | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Ongoing medicines (monthly refills, e.g. blood pressure) | 75% | Low -- can be planned |
| Urgent new prescriptions (e.g. antibiotics after a doctor visit) | 25% | High -- needed today |

**Exhibit 2 — Costs per City**

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Setup cost per city (local licensed facility) | about $4M |
| Running cost per city per year | about $3M |

**Exhibit 3 — Results in Existing Same-Day Cities**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Customers who moved all their prescriptions to Amazon after using same-day for an urgent prescription | 35% |
| Same metric in cities without same-day (customers who filled an urgent prescription elsewhere) | 8% |
| Urgent prescriptions per year per city that Amazon could capture | about 60,000 |

## D. Model answer / "what good looks like"

**Framework a strong candidate should build:**
1. Clarify the goal and understand customer needs (urgent vs. ongoing).
2. Recognize where same-day matters: urgent prescriptions -- the moment customers currently go to a local drugstore.
3. Quantify the value per city vs. the cost.
4. Recommend a rollout sequence with criteria.

**Quantitative reasoning a strong candidate shows:**
- Urgent prescriptions captured per city: 60,000. Extra customers who move everything: 60,000 x (35% - 8%) = about 16,200.
- Value: 16,200 x $600 = about $9.7M of gross profit a year per city (growing as customers stay).
- Cost: $4M setup + $3M a year -> pays back within the first year in a typical city.

**Correct core insight:** Same-day isn't valuable for the 75% of planned refills -- two-day delivery works for them. Its value is winning the urgent moment, which is when customers switch pharmacies: after an urgent same-day fill, 35% move all their prescriptions to Amazon vs. 8% otherwise. That makes same-day an acquisition tool for the whole pharmacy relationship, with a fast payback -- as long as there's enough urgent volume in a city.

**A strong final recommendation includes:**
- Expand, but sequenced: start with the cities with the most urgent-prescription volume (where the per-city math is strongest), given licensing lead times.
- Focus the experience on urgent prescriptions (e.g. connection from doctor visits), then move customers to planned delivery for refills.
- Metrics: urgent prescriptions captured, share of customers moving all prescriptions, payback per city.
- Risks: licensing timelines, operational quality, pharmacist staffing.

**Scoring anchors:**
- 5/5 business sense: identifies urgency as the key, uses the switching data, quantifies value per city vs. cost, and recommends a sequenced rollout.
- 3/5: recommends expansion with some numbers, but misses the urgent vs. ongoing distinction.
- 1/5: recommends expanding everywhere because customers like speed, or rejects it as too expensive without math.
