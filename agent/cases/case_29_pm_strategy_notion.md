# Case File 29 — Notion: How to Grow into Large Enterprises (PM Strategy & Growth Case)

## A. Case prompt (read aloud to candidate at the start — nothing else revealed yet)

"You're a PM on Notion's strategy team. Notion grew mostly bottom-up: individuals and small teams adopt it, then their companies pay. Leadership now wants a bigger share of very large enterprises -- companies with ten thousand or more employees -- where established tools dominate. They're debating how to go after this market. What would you recommend, and how would you approach the decision?"

**Objective (private reference, reveal only if asked directly):** Recommend a strategy for winning large enterprises, grounded in Notion's right to win and the economics of different go-to-market approaches.

---

## B. Clarifying-question answer key

| # | Likely question | Answer | Reveal rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Why does leadership want large enterprises now?" | Large enterprises have bigger, stickier contracts, and growth from small teams is slowing. | On request |
| 2 | "What's Notion's presence in large enterprises today?" | Reveal Exhibit 1 once asked about existing presence, usage, or footprint. | On request |
| 3 | "What do large enterprises need that Notion lacks?" | Their security reviews usually require advanced admin controls, audit logs, data residency options, and integrations with their identity systems. Notion has some of these, but gaps block about a third of security reviews. | On request |
| 4 | "Who are the competitors?" | Established wiki and document tools that are already deployed company-wide, often bundled with other software the enterprise buys. | On request |
| 5 | "What are the economics of different sales approaches?" | Reveal Exhibit 2 once asked about sales approaches, cost, conversion, or sales cycles. | On request |
| 6 | "What does a typical enterprise contract look like?" | Average annual contract value for enterprises of this size is about $250,000. | On request |
| 7 | "Where did our existing enterprise deals come from?" | About 60% of existing large-enterprise contracts started from teams already using Notion inside the company. | On request |

**Guardrail reminder:** Do not tell the candidate whether to go top-down or bottom-up, or which gaps to close first -- let them find the right-to-win argument from the data.

## C. Exhibits / data

**Exhibit 1 — Notion Footprint in Large Enterprises (10k+ employees)**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Large enterprises with at least 50 active Notion users but no company-wide contract | 4,000 |
| Large enterprises with a company-wide contract | 600 |
| Average active users in the 4,000 "unofficial" footprints | 180 |

**Exhibit 2 — Sales Approach Comparison (last year's pilots)**

| Approach | Win rate | Sales cycle | Cost to win a deal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outbound to enterprises with no Notion usage | 3% | about 11 months | about $180,000 |
| Sales engagement with enterprises that already have 50+ active users | 25% | about 4 months | about $40,000 |

## D. Model answer / "what good looks like"

**Framework a strong candidate should build:**
1. Clarify the goal and Notion's current position.
2. Find the right to win: where Notion has an advantage over established tools.
3. Compare go-to-market approaches on win rate, cycle time, and cost.
4. Identify product gaps that block deals.
5. Recommend a sequenced strategy and metrics.

**Correct core insight:** Notion's advantage in large enterprises is the usage it already has: 4,000 enterprises have unofficial footprints of ~180 users each. Selling into those converts at 25% in 4 months at ~$40K cost, versus 3% in 11 months at ~$180K going in cold. The strategy is to turn existing bottom-up usage into company-wide contracts (product-led sales), and to close the security and admin gaps that block a third of reviews -- not to compete head-on for enterprises with no Notion usage.

**Quantitative reasoning a strong candidate shows:**
- Opportunity in existing footprints: 4,000 x 25% = 1,000 deals x $250K = about $250M in annual contract value.
- Unit economics: $250K contract vs. $40K cost to win (pays back fast) vs. $180K for cold outbound with a 3% win rate.
- Security gaps blocking ~1/3 of reviews suggest closing them could raise the win rate further.

**A strong final recommendation includes:**
- Prioritize product-led sales into the 4,000 enterprises with existing usage, using usage signals to pick accounts.
- Invest in the enterprise-readiness gaps (admin, audit, data residency, identity) that block security reviews.
- Keep outbound to "no usage" enterprises limited until win rates improve.
- Metrics: conversion of footprints to contracts, sales cycle, security-review pass rate, expansion after contract.

**Scoring anchors:**
- 5/5 business sense: identifies existing footprints as the right to win, compares the approaches with numbers, sizes the opportunity, and names the security gaps as an enabler.
- 3/5: recommends a reasonable strategy but without using the footprint or sales economics data.
- 1/5: recommends hiring a big enterprise sales team to go after all large companies, or building every feature competitors have, without analysis.
